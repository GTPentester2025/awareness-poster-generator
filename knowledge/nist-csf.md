# NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0

## Key facts
- NIST CSF 2.0 (publication CSWP 29) was released on 26 February 2024 — the first major update since the framework's 2014 debut.
- It is designed for ANY organization — regardless of size, sector, or maturity — not just critical infrastructure.
- CSF 2.0 organizes cybersecurity outcomes into six Functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover.
- "Govern" is the newest Function, added in 2.0 to put risk management strategy and leadership accountability at the center.
- The CSF describes desired outcomes, not a checklist — it does not prescribe how outcomes must be achieved.
- It is voluntary, free, and translated into many languages; it maps to standards like ISO 27001 and NIST SP 800-53.
- The framework's components are the Core (Functions/Categories/Subcategories), Profiles, and Tiers.
- Profiles let an organization compare its "current" security posture with its "target" posture to plan improvements.
- Tiers (1 Partial to 4 Adaptive) characterize how rigorously an organization governs and manages cyber risk.
- NIST publishes free Quick-Start Guides, including ones for small businesses, to help organizations adopt CSF 2.0.

## The six functions in plain language
- GOVERN (GV) — set the strategy: who is responsible, what the policies are, and how cyber risk decisions are made and monitored.
- IDENTIFY (ID) — know what you have: understand your assets (data, hardware, software, people, suppliers) and their risks.
- PROTECT (PR) — lock things down: use safeguards (access control, training, encryption, patching) to prevent or limit harm.
- DETECT (DE) — spot trouble fast: find and analyze anomalies and indicators that an attack may be underway.
- RESPOND (RS) — act on incidents: contain the damage, manage the incident, and communicate with stakeholders.
- RECOVER (RC) — bounce back: restore affected systems and operations, and communicate during recovery.

## Who it's for
- Industry, government agencies, nonprofits, and other organizations of every size and sector.
- Executives and boards — the Govern function speaks directly to leadership accountability.
- Security teams — the Core gives a shared taxonomy for assessing, prioritizing and communicating security work.
- Small businesses — a dedicated CSF 2.0 Small Business Quick-Start Guide exists.

## Framework history
- 2014 — CSF 1.0 published, originally aimed at US critical infrastructure (under Executive Order 13636).
- April 2018 — CSF 1.1 update added supply chain risk management and identity management detail.
- 8 August 2023 — CSF 2.0 public draft released for comment.
- 26 February 2024 — CSF 2.0 final published as NIST CSWP 29, expanding scope to all organizations.

## How organizations use it
- Assess: map current practices against the CSF Core to find gaps.
- Prioritize: build a Target Profile reflecting business needs, risk appetite, and requirements.
- Communicate: give executives, auditors and partners a common language for cyber risk.
- Improve: track progress from Current Profile to Target Profile over time.
- Connect: use NIST's online Informative References to link CSF outcomes to specific controls (e.g. SP 800-53).

## Memorable framing for posters
- Six verbs to run a security program: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover.
- Security is a cycle, not a one-time project — the functions operate continuously and concurrently.
- You can't protect what you haven't identified; you can't respond to what you don't detect.
- Cybersecurity is a leadership issue: governance sits at the heart of CSF 2.0's function wheel.
- One framework, every organization: from a corner shop to a federal agency.
- Know it, guard it, watch it, answer it, restore it — and govern it all.
- Free to use, globally recognized, and built to fit the standards you already follow.
- Start small: NIST's quick-start guides turn the framework into a first checklist.
